\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}


The experiments comparing the use of \acrlong{abb} with \acrlong{obb} show interesting results. \acrshortpl{obb} run more precisely along the object boundaries and capture less surrounding area, which in theory should lead to greater accuracy in object detection. This is reflected in the smaller surface areas of the bounding boxes. Surprisingly, however, the ABB model shows better performance in terms of \Acrlong{mAP}. One possible explanation is that small deviations in the orientation of the \acrshort{obb} can greatly reduce the overlap with the \Acrlong{GT}. Even minimal angular deviations lead to significantly lower intersection-over-union values, which reduces the \acrshort{mAP} of the \acrshort{obb} models. \acrshortpl{abb} thus offers more stable performance, while \acrshortpl{obb} adapts the bounding boxes more accurately to the object and can therefore use computing resources more efficiently. In the discussion, any reference to \acrshort{mAP} specifically refers to the \acrshort{mAP}@0.5--0.95 value.
 
Analysis of channel permutations shows that the choice of spectral channels significantly affects object detection performance. Models that use the \acrshort{IR} channel in combination with \acrshort{RGB} achieve the highest mAP (0.59–0.63), while the blue channel makes only a limited contribution. This can be attributed to the fact that the blue spectral range (420–490 nm) has higher spectral selectivity compared to red (650–780 nm), green (490–575 nm) and infrared (>800 nm) \cite{bfs_Strahlung}. At the same time, vehicles stand out less from their surroundings in this wavelength range because asphalt is highly reflective in the blue spectrum \cite{Singh2013}; a similar effect could apply to vehicle paintwork in corresponding colours. \\
The \acrshort{NDVI} channel reduces model performance, presumably due to additional noise in the dataset. Vehicles can be reliably detected even without \acrshort{NDVI}, as the differentiation of vegetation provides no clear advantage for detection and may even impair model performance. It is conceivable that the spatial context, as captured by the \acrshort{NDVI}, contributes to certain disturbances in the dataset.

The investigation of the primary permutations shows that the IRGB model performs the worst. Models such as \acrshort{RGBIR} benefit from the combination of true colour channels and \acrshort{IR}, while \acrshort{NDVI} reduces detection performance. All models show difficulties in separating background and objects, especially for the classes \textit{Ship} and \textit{Van}. \textit{Planes}, on the other hand, are reliably detected, presumably due to their characteristic shape and unique environment, such as airports or water surfaces.
 
The ablation studies confirm that individual channels have only a limited influence on detection performance. Only the combination of several channels leads to a significant improvement in \acrshort{mAP}. Single channels achieve values below \acrshort{mAP}: 0.55, while combinations such as RGIR achieve values between 0.59 and 0.63. Minimal fluctuations in the quantiles between the individual channels indicate that even a single channel (except \acrshort{NDVI}) is fundamentally suitable for object recognition (see. Fig. \ref{fig:ablation_map50-95:val_on_test}). The \acrshort{NDVI} channel delivers the worst performance at \acrshort{mAP}: 0.42–0.44, which cannot be explained by the radiometric resolution (8 bits). Vehicles of the class \textit{Car} are robustly recognised, even without channel combination, presumably due to the high amount of training data and their characteristic shape. The greatest differences occur with background objects, with IR delivering the best results and combinations of channels enabling further improvements. Difficulties arise in particular with generic classes such as \textit{Vehicle} and with confusion between \textit{Ship} and \textit{Camping Car}, as the shape or partial coverage of the objects makes differentiation difficult. Distinguishing between individual vehicle types other than \textit{Car} remains a challenge due to limited data.

Against the backdrop of these results, it can be concluded that the use of \acrshort{DL}-based methods to generate suggestions for object detection and classification in vehicles has the potential to reduce the workload of human analysts. At the same time, the results show that the human factor continues to be of central importance. Compared to human analysts, the models tend to make incorrect classifications or fail to reliably identify relevant objects in a considerable number of cases. This highlights both the limitations of current model quality and the need for a hybrid approach in which algorithmic suggestions are reviewed and validated by human expertise.

In addition to these model-intrinsic limitations, scene-specific factors also have a significant impact on detection performance. Vehicles in structured suburban scenes are relatively easy to recognise, while vehicles in scrap yards or overgrown areas are difficult to detect. Colour also plays a role: white vehicles on grey asphalt are easily recognised, while grey or black vehicles are more difficult to distinguish from the background. This correlates with the distribution of vehicle colours in the USA, where white (24\%) and black (23\%) are particularly common \cite{abc_utah}.


\acrshort{YOLO} models are well suited for vehicle detection and, to a limited extent, for more complex multi-class scenarios, provided that the spatial resolution and the number of spectral channels are sufficient. In addition, vehicle types must be clearly distinguishable in terms of shape, colour, and size to avoid misclassification. With high-resolution data (12.5~\texttimes~12.5 cm per pixel), reliable detection of different vehicle classes is achievable, particularly for cars, which achieve a recall of over 80\%. This performance can likely be attributed to the large amount of training data. \acrshort{obb} correspond to the definition of \textit{very small objects} according to \citeauthor{Chen2017} \cite{Chen2017}, enabling the detection of small objects with \acrshort{YOLO}v9.

\acrshort{YOLO}v9 achieves a \acrshort{mAP} of approximately 43\% on \acrshort{DOTA}, representing a substantial improvement over \acrshort{YOLO}v3, which, as reported by \citeauthor{Balzer2022} \cite{Balzer2022}, attains nearly 0\% on the test data and only around 10\% on the training data. In the present work, the \acrshort{YOLO}v3-based approach trained on the \acrshort{DOTA} dataset achieved an average performance of 0.34 on the validation data. The discrepancy with the results of \citeauthor{Balzer2022} may be explained by architectural modifications in the earlier study (e.g., the addition of a dropout layer to YOLOv3), suboptimal preprocessing, or the use of the Ultralytics \acrshort{YOLO}v3u implementation in this thesis. In comparison, \acrshort{YOLO}v9 and \acrshort{YOLO}v12 achieved \acrshort{mAP} values of 0.42 and 0.40, respectively. The superior performance of \acrshort{YOLO}v9 can be attributed to its modified architecture and longer training. 

The comparison between pre-trained and trained-\textit{from-scratch} models shows that the \acrshort{YOLO} version itself has only a minor impact on performance. Across all three spectral permutations, the trained-\textit{from-scratch} \acrshort{YOLO}v9 models perform as well as or better than the one-year newer \acrshort{YOLO}v12 models, which may be due to limited evolutionary changes and architectural developments between successive model cycles in 2024 and 2025.

For both phenomena—the slightly lower validation performance of \acrshort{YOLO}v12 on \acrshort{DOTA} and the comparison between scratch-trained \acrshort{YOLO}v9 and \acrshort{YOLO}v12 models—the underlying reason may lie in the implementation of the \acrshort{AA2} methodology, which shifts the focus towards real-time object detection and classification.

\acrshort{YOLO}v9 is capable of reliably localizing and categorizing \Acrlong{GT} objects, which enables applications such as the analysis of vehicle distributions across entire regions, especially in combination with regular satellite overflights \cite{planet_labs, airbus_neo}. Similar approaches have been applied, for example, to satellite imagery of Chinese crematoria during the COVID-19 pandemic to verify official mortality data by evaluating the utilization of parking spaces for hearses. In combination with corroborating sources, such analyses can provide additional insights into official statistics \cite{Spiegel_article}.

The use of multispectral channels and spatial context information offers several advantages. The model can recognise typical patterns, such as car parks or port areas, and provide semantic information from multispectral channels, which makes the classification more robust against camouflage, shadows or colour variations. Channels such as \acrshort{NDVI} or \acrshort{IR} also enable the assessment of object condition and use. The combination of multiple channels improves generalisation to different environments such as urban, forest or desert, thus increasing robustness to new test areas. Beyond pure vehicle detection, categories such as vehicle type (based on shape and size), condition (colour, e.g. rust detection) or usage context (area classification, car in densely populated urban environment is more likely than aircraft) can be distinguished. Combining this with other data sources, such as \acrshort{SAR}, \acrshort{LiDAR} or elevation models, enables applications such as traffic density analysis, infrastructure condition or usage patterns.
